# Why Prove It Yourself?

*If AI can generate a proof of almost anything, why do people still gather on sites like Math Stack Exchange to work through proofs together — and why does anyone still bother writing one by hand?*

I started answering questions on Math Stack Exchange this summer, and the longer I do it, the more the question nags at me. For most problems posted there, a decent AI can produce a plausible proof in seconds. So what is the platform for? What am I for, sitting there polishing an argument a machine could have drafted before my page finished loading?

I don't think the honest answer is "AI isn't good enough yet." For routine problems, it mostly is. The real answer is that a proof was never just text. Several distinct things are bundled into that single word, and AI unbundled them.

## Generation got cheap. Verification didn't.

Producing proof-shaped text is now close to free. Checking it is not. A generated proof can be fluent, confident, and wrong in exactly one step — and finding that step takes the same skill it always did. The bottleneck didn't disappear; it shifted from *writing* to *checking*. In that world, any community whose real product is checked work becomes more important, not less.

## An answer is a signature

When I post an answer under my name, I am not merely releasing text. I am saying: I stand behind this, and if it's wrong, it costs me — reputation, visibly, in public. A model can't do that. Being wrong costs it nothing.

That accountability is not decoration. It is the mechanism that lets a stranger trust an argument without re-verifying every line, and it is why a page with a name, a history, and a vote count carries information a raw chatbot answer lacks. The trail matters: who answered, what was corrected, and what the comments pushed back on.

## The figuring-out is the point

Most people on a Q&A site are not there merely to extract a final answer. Askers routinely arrive having already half-solved their problem in the comments; what they want is to think it through in public, with resistance. Answering works the same way from the other side. Writing an argument for strangers — using every hypothesis, declaring every gap, surviving the comments — is training that reading a finished solution never provides. When a machine silently hands you the final text, you get the artifact and skip the part that changes you.

## A proof is understanding, not just certification

Mathematicians don't prove things merely to be sure they're true. The proof is where the understanding lives: it's what lets you extend a result, adapt it, and see why it breaks when a hypothesis is dropped. And before any of that, someone has to choose the question, the definition, the frame. That choice is taste, and taste remains stubbornly human. AI optimizes inside whatever frame it is handed.

## What I concede

The low end of these platforms is genuinely being absorbed. Routine homework questions no longer need a forum, and pretending otherwise is nostalgia. Traffic will shift, some rituals will die, and that's fine.

What survives is the high end: hard questions, judgment, teaching, and an archive of work checked by identifiable people. (It's worth remembering where the AI's training data came from.)

## Coda

In most of what I build, I'm interested in systems that make uncertainty visible rather than papering over it with confident output. A Q&A community is that idea in social form: claims attached to names, with the corrections left in public.

So here is the short answer to my own question: AI made generating answers cheap. It did not make *mattering who checked them* cheap, and it did not make *learning by doing it yourself* obsolete. That's why the platforms persist, and it's why I still write the proofs.
